# User research 

## Target audience
My target audience is first-generation undergraduate students in the United States. A lot of the tools and organizations that I will be talking about through my tool also have to do with graduate students, but my main focus is first-generation undergraduate students. This whole tool is basically a commercial for my first-generation student college tool called the Layan Library, where it's going to have tech-based tools and a database of organizations that can help them. Specifically for this part, I'm going to be focusing on the financial aspect of it and telling a story about how first-generation college students need information about financials specifically related to college. The data has shown they graduate with more debt than continuing generation students, so the information gap definitely is there. For identifying people to talk to, I mostly wanted to talk to students at Heinz because the majority of them are first-generation college students. I just wanted to see whether the story I am trying to tell my project that it is clear and they can clearly identify the types of resources that I'm making through my tool. My approach for finding representatives to interview was just talking to the other people in my class. They would be able to offer me good insights because they are my peers and they are currently students. 

## Interview script


Goal: Whether the story is clear
Questions to Ask:
- Do the visuals tell a clear story?
- Are the visuals and graphics easy to read?
- Is this too much information being thrown at you at once?

Goal: Was the project aesthetically good?
Questions to Ask: 
- Is the color scheme making it easier or harder to follow the story?
- Would you have preferred a color scheme with less colors?

Goal: Whether, through this story, they understand why the tool I am building is helpful
Questions to Ask:
- Do you understand why this tool was created?
- Do you understand the gap in financial knowledge between first-gen and continuing gen students?

## Interview findings
I talked to two students in my class about whether they were easily able to follow the story, whether the project was aesthetically pleasing, and whether the aesthetics got in the way of telling the story or not. They basically said that they were able to follow the story. I had everything out in cohesive steps where they were able to follow it. Basically, what I said with the story I started with a scenario. There are two students, Laia and Elias. Laia ended up graduating college with a significant amount of debt, and Elias graduated with minimal debt. The fun fact about Laia and Elias is that they both went to the same school, they both had the same major, they had similar grades, everything was the same, so how did one student end up in debt and the other didn't? Then I got into how Laia is one of the 8.2 million first-generation college students in the United States. 

I then showed data about how first-gen students are in more debt and the ratio of where they go to school, whether that's community college, a state school, a private school, or another sort of institution. I also showed that, despite the majority of first-gen students starting at community college and stuff like that, they ended up in more debt. This is an information gap issue because there were also reports on how, in the conversation about scholarships, it only happens around 30% of the time with first-gen students, but it's 70% of the time with continuing-gen students. I am going to be releasing this tool that really talks about the information gap and the tools and the organizations that could help these students gain access to the information that they need to better their own college and career experience.

Overall, the two students that I talked to said that my story was pretty clear. Specifically, for the tables where I was talking about the types of schools, thry said I should have more vivid titles and to fix the font to make the visuals more clear. Make sure that the aesthetic is really cohesive with only a couple of colors. Introducing different types of colors for the visuals can get distracting. I should have a solid script on what I'm going to say, so I could get that description down to 1 minute. I should also look into possibly making, instead of using visuals for statistics, just putting words on paper for the stats. My professor has also talked about how I could compare the population of first-gen students to a specific population (for example, relating this to the population of New York or the population of LA) to put into context how many first-gen students are in the United States. They also said that, through the one-minute pitch that I was telling them with my visualizations, they could see the gap between first-generation students and continuing gen students. 


# Identified changes for Part III
So overall, for part three, I will be using Lovable to make my presentation. There's going to be one Lovable link that's specifically for my actual tool, where it's going to go through the story quickly and concisely. This will serve as the about page that users can refer to understand why this tool was made. The formal project that I'll be turning in for this class will also be on Lovable, but it'll be a little bit longer with more explanation because I won't be presenting it verbally. It will also have proper links to citations. 

Based on the feedback that I got, instead of making a waffle chart to show that 54% of students in the United States are first gen, I'm going to focus more on the 8.4 million number and how there are that many first-gen students in the United States. I'm also going to decrease the number of colors that I'm using in my project and make the tables easier to read. I will also make sure to add more specific titles to my tables so the viewer can immediately know what I am trying to get across with my data visualizations.
